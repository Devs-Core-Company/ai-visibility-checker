Electric or manual toothbrush?

By Tom Ellis, Dental therapist · Published 15 April 2025 · Updated 1 June 2025

Key points from this article

  • What the research shows: Reviews of clinical trials find that powered brushes with an oscillating-rotating head remove a little more plaque and reduce gum inflammation slightly more than manual brushes over three months and longer.
  • Features worth paying for: A two-minute timer and a pressure sensor are the most useful features.
  • When manual is fine: A manual brush used well, twice a day for two minutes with fluoride toothpaste, keeps teeth healthy.
  • People who benefit most: Electric brushes help people with limited hand movement, such as arthritis, children learning to brush, and people with braces or many fillings and crowns.

What the research shows

Reviews of clinical trials find that powered brushes with an oscillating-rotating head remove a little more plaque and reduce gum inflammation slightly more than manual brushes over three months and longer.

Features worth paying for

A two-minute timer and a pressure sensor are the most useful features. App connectivity and multiple cleaning modes add cost without clear benefit.

When manual is fine

A manual brush used well, twice a day for two minutes with fluoride toothpaste, keeps teeth healthy. Technique matters more than the brush.

People who benefit most

Electric brushes help people with limited hand movement, such as arthritis, children learning to brush, and people with braces or many fillings and crowns.
