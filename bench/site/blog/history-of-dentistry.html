A short history of dentistry in Britain

By Dr Amelia Hart, Principal dentist · Published 8 July 2026

For most of British history people with toothache went to whoever would pull the tooth. In medieval towns that was usually the barber-surgeon, who also cut hair, let blood and set bones. Travelling tooth-drawers worked at fairs and markets, often with a drummer to cover the noise. There was little understanding of why teeth decayed, and extraction was the only reliable treatment.

Sugar changed the picture. As cane sugar became cheaper from the seventeenth century onwards, decay became far more common, first among the wealthy and later across society. Portraits of the period rarely show open-mouthed smiles, and contemporary writers comment on the blackened teeth of fashionable people. Demand grew for people who could do more than extract.

In the eighteenth century a small number of practitioners began to describe themselves as dentists. They fitted dentures made of ivory, bone or human teeth, sometimes taken from soldiers killed in battle; dentures made after the Battle of Waterloo were known for decades as Waterloo teeth. Fillings of lead, tin and later gold were used in wealthier patients.

The nineteenth century brought two major advances. Anaesthesia, first with nitrous oxide and then ether and chloroform, made extractions bearable. Vulcanised rubber made dentures cheaper and more comfortable. Dental schools opened in London, and in 1878 the Dentists Act created a register of qualified practitioners, although unregistered practice remained legal for decades.

The Dentists Act of 1921 finally restricted practice to registered dentists. Many unqualified practitioners already working were allowed onto the register, which meant standards varied widely. Fluoride toothpaste was decades away, and most adults expected to lose all their teeth by middle age. Full clearances, in which all remaining teeth were removed at once, were common wedding or twenty-first birthday presents in some parts of the country.

The National Health Service, launched in July 1948, made dental treatment free at the point of use. Demand was overwhelming. In the first year dentists fitted millions of dentures, and the cost was far higher than planned. Charges for dentures were introduced in 1951, and charges for other treatment followed. The structure of NHS dental contracts has been changed several times since.

Prevention transformed the second half of the century. Fluoride toothpaste became widely available in the 1970s, and some areas added fluoride to the water supply. The proportion of adults with no natural teeth fell from over a third in 1968 to around six per cent by 2009. Dental hygienists, first trained in the 1940s, became central to keeping gums healthy.

Today most adults keep most of their teeth for life. Implants, adhesive fillings, digital scanning and clear aligners are routine in many practices. The main challenges have shifted to access to care, gum disease in an ageing population, and the steady problem of sugar in the national diet, which remains the root cause of decay as it was three hundred years ago.
